The Hook: 40%+ inflation is destroying Iranian household savings. No platform combines investment + insurance + banking. I'm defining the product strategy and architecture for the first one — building a trust framework that makes investing feel as safe as saving, with AI-augmented discovery compressing 8 weeks into 3.

Executive Summary

I'm leading the design of a consumer digital investment platform for Pasargad Insurance — a product that lets ordinary people invest in gold, silver, stocks, bonds, real estate, and fixed income funds, with life insurance embedded invisibly into every transaction.

The project exists because 40%+ annual inflation in Iran is destroying household savings, and Pasargad has a unique ecosystem advantage: a licensed bank (Vipod), an insurance company, and investment infrastructure — all under one umbrella. No competitor has all three.

I own the full design lifecycle: competitive intelligence, persona development, experience mapping, product strategy, MVP definition, information architecture, interaction design, and design system foundations — as the sole product designer.

The Context

Pasargad Insurance is one of Iran's largest insurers. Its subsidiary Vipod is a licensed digital bank. The investment arm manages multiple fund types. These three entities operate independently — but the opportunity is in connecting them into a single consumer experience.

The user context is critical: most Iranians keep savings in bank deposits earning 23% — while inflation runs at 40%+. They lose purchasing power every month. Gold has historically been the hedge, but buying physical gold has friction (trust, storage, minimum amounts). Digital gold solves these problems — but no existing platform combines investment + insurance + banking in one seamless experience.

The product complexity is significant: 9 investment fund types, each with different risk profiles, return characteristics, and regulatory requirements. Life insurance with variable coverage. Banking integration for instant deposits and withdrawals. Regulatory compliance across three financial domains (insurance, securities, banking).

The Opportunity

Why now: Competitor Karizama proved the model — 1M+ users investing in digital gold through a simple app. But Karizama has no banking license (slow withdrawals) and no insurance (no protection). Pasargad can offer 5-second identity verification via Vipod SSO, same-day withdrawals, and automatic life insurance — three structural advantages no competitor can replicate.

Why previous solutions couldn't scale: Pasargad's existing investment products were sold through agents and paper forms. The digital channel didn't exist for retail consumers. There was nothing to redesign — this was building from zero.

Business risks: If the experience is too complex, users won't trust it. If insurance feels like a "cost" rather than a benefit, it becomes a barrier. If onboarding takes more than 3 minutes, we lose the users Karizama already trained to expect simplicity.

Problem Statement

"How do we make investment + insurance feel like one simple act — not two separate financial decisions — for users who have never invested digitally and distrust financial promises?"
  • Design Challenge: Make 9 fund types + variable insurance + regulatory disclosures feel simple and trustworthy — not overwhelming
  • Product Challenge: Build a 0→1 product that serves conservative first-timers and sophisticated investors with the same flow
  • Engineering Challenge: Integrate three backend systems (insurance, banking, investment) into a seamless frontend experience
  • Business Challenge: Position insurance as a value-add, not a cost — overcoming deep cultural skepticism about life insurance

Strategic Discovery

Competitive Analysis — 8 Players, 3 Layers

Analyzed competitors across three market layers:

  • Direct insurance competitors: Pasargad Life existing products, Saman Insurance, Novin Insurance — traditional, paper-heavy, no digital investment
  • Digital investment apps: Karizama, Milli, Agah — simple investment but no insurance, no banking integration
  • Broader fintech: International references (Acorns, Wealthsimple, Lemonade) — product patterns and trust-building strategies

Key finding: No competitor offers transparent fee breakdown. This became the biggest differentiation opportunity.

Persona Development — 3 Data-Backed Profiles

  • Sina (Primary): 35, married, IT employee. Conservative. Wants to protect savings. His wife is the real decision-maker — needs "proof" that this is safe.
  • Niloofar: 28, freelancer, digital-native. Wants growth. Comfortable with risk but skeptical of traditional insurance.
  • Hossein: 48, existing Pasargad policyholder. Disappointed with returns on traditional insurance. Open to digital but needs handholding.

Experience Mapping

Built a complete 7-phase × 8-layer Experience Map: Awareness → Research → Evaluation → Decision → Onboarding → Usage → Loyalty. Each phase mapped with: triggers, actions, thoughts, emotions, pain points, opportunities, touchpoints, and KPIs.

Critical insight: The lowest emotional point is the Evaluation phase — when the user's spouse asks "Is this like Caspian?" (referring to a collapsed financial institution). The entire trust architecture of the product must address this moment.

Principles

These principles guided every design decision — they're product principles, not UI rules:

  • Invisible Insurance: Insurance is a benefit that activates automatically — never a separate purchase decision. Users don't "buy insurance" — they invest, and protection comes included.
  • Transparency as Competitive Advantage: Show the fee breakdown (94% investment / 4% insurance / 2% platform) on every screen. No competitor does this. Radical transparency builds the trust others lack.
  • The Spouse Test: Every key screen must produce output shareable with a skeptical family member. If Sina can't screenshot the calculator result and convince his wife, we've failed.
  • Start Small, Prove Big: ₹100K minimum (~$2). Remove the risk of the first decision. Once the first investment is made, Endowment Effect activates.
  • System, Not Screens: Design the product as a modular system — every component reusable, every flow extensible. We're building infrastructure for 9 fund types, not designing 9 separate products.

Strategy

Chosen approach: Guided personalization with progressive complexity. Users answer 2 questions (goal + risk tolerance), receive a personalized package recommendation, but can customize freely.

Alternative considered: Full self-service (user picks funds manually from day one). Rejected because: Sina doesn't know what "fixed income fund" means. Choice overload with 9 fund types would cause decision paralysis (Hick's Law). Karizama succeeded precisely because they simplified to one choice.

Alternative considered: Fully automated (system decides everything). Rejected because: users need Autonomy (Self-Determination Theory). A system that decides for you feels paternalistic. Users who feel in control are more loyal.

Trade-off: The 2-question Risk Quiz sacrifices precision for completion. A 10-question assessment would be more accurate but lose 60%+ of users (NNg research: each question adds 10-15% drop-off). We optimize for good-enough personalization with near-zero friction.

Product Architecture

The product is designed as a layered system, not a collection of screens:

Layer 1: Trust Architecture

Calculator before signup (prove value before asking for commitment). Fee transparency on every screen. Regulatory badges (Central Insurance, Securities Exchange). SSO via Vipod (institutional trust transfer).

Layer 2: Personalization Engine

Risk Quiz (2 questions) → 12 profile combinations → Package recommendation algorithm → 3 packages displayed with Anchoring (recommended in center). Custom Portfolio Builder for advanced users. Guardrails: minimum 10% fixed income, maximum 45% stocks.

Layer 3: Investment Operations

Purchase flow. DCA (automatic recurring investment). Fund switching without sell/rebuy. Same-day withdrawal to Vipod account. Real-time dashboard with daily P&L.

Layer 4: Insurance Layer (Invisible)

Auto-activates on first purchase. Adjustable coverage via slider. Premium included in fee breakdown — never a separate line item. Death and disability coverage. Scales with investment amount.

Layer 5: Engagement & Growth

Smart notifications (positive-only on green days). Monthly comparison report (vs. deposit, vs. inflation). Referral program (gold rewards). New fund type launches as expansion.

Solution Evolution

Iteration 1: Feature-First

Initial wireframes focused on showing all 9 fund types upfront. User testing signal: overwhelming. Users didn't know where to start.

Iteration 2: Guided Personalization

Introduced Risk Quiz → Package recommendation. Reduced 9 choices to 3. Added Anchoring (center = recommended). Signal: much better comprehension, but users wanted to understand "why this package?"

Iteration 3: Transparency + Control

Added fee breakdown to every screen. Added Custom Portfolio Builder for advanced users. Added "worst case" scenario to calculator (not just optimistic). Current state — resonates with both Sina (conservative) and Niloofar (control-seeking).

Reflection

What surprised me

The biggest insight wasn't about UI — it was that Sina is not the decision-maker. His wife is. Every trust-building feature (calculator, transparency, shareable results) exists because of this insight. I would have missed it without the experience mapping process.

What I'd challenge now

I accepted the 2-question Risk Quiz too quickly. The simplicity is right for conversion — but I'm not convinced the personalization output is meaningful enough. If I could revisit one decision, I'd explore whether a 4-question flow could preserve completion while producing meaningfully different recommendations. The current 12-profile matrix may be over-engineered for what two inputs can actually differentiate.

What remains uncertain

The invisible insurance positioning is strategically sound — but we haven't validated whether users actually perceive the protection as valuable when they can't see it. There's a real risk that users minimize the insurance slider to zero because they view the 4% as "waste." We won't know until we have real behavioral data.

How this changed my thinking

This project fundamentally changed how I think about AI as a design tool. AI didn't replace my judgment — it amplified my capacity. But every output needed human validation. The future of product design isn't AI or human — it's AI-augmented human judgment.

Product Design — Process, Impact & Selected Screens

"The hardest design problem in this product is not the interface. It's making a family feel safe putting their savings somewhere new."

The Hook: Stakeholders asked me to fix a form. I convinced them to rebuild the entire issuance infrastructure instead. Completion went from ~58% to ~73%, validation errors dropped ~45%, and the organization shipped its first UX governance framework. The system now serves 3 insurance lines from one modular architecture.

Context

The platform supported multiple insurance lines (Motor, Property, Health), each with distinct regulatory requirements, pricing logic, and risk assessment rules. Over time, issuance flows had evolved independently per product line. While functional, they became structurally inconsistent, hard to scale, dependent on UX for minor changes, and increasingly error-prone.

Business objective: Improve completion rate while enabling faster rollout of new insurance products without increasing operational overhead.

The initial stakeholder hypothesis was "too many steps." However, after conducting funnel analysis, scroll and rage-click heatmaps, and 10 focused user interviews, a deeper pattern emerged.

The core issue was not step count — it was structural inconsistency and cognitive overload. Users struggled with fragmented financial information, inconsistent interaction patterns between policy types, and unclear cause-effect relationships between inputs and final premium.

"The experience lacked systemic coherence. Each policy type felt like a different product built by a different team."

Strategic Reframing

Instead of redesigning a single issuance flow, I proposed shifting from a flow-level solution to a structural solution: build a modular issuance framework that would standardize decision logic, unify interaction behaviors, enable reuse across policy types, and reduce long-term UX dependency.

This reframed the initiative from "optimize one flow" to "build scalable issuance infrastructure."

Core Design Decisions

Flow Re-architecture

We introduced progressive step grouping, a persistent financial summary panel, and real-time premium recalculation. This allowed users to immediately understand how each input impacted total cost, reducing uncertainty and cognitive friction.

Standardized Input & Validation Logic

Unified validation rules, inline contextual error messages, context-aware tooltips, and smart defaults based on historical data — reducing repetitive user errors and lowering correction loops.

Dynamic Cost Transparency Module

A structured cost breakdown module including base premium, risk adjustments, discounts, and taxes. The visual "part-to-whole" breakdown improved financial clarity and perceived trust.

From Flow Optimization to Design Systemization

To prevent recurrence of inconsistency, I extracted the flow into reusable building blocks: issuance-specific components, behavioral state definitions, conditional logic documentation, and decision trees for product variations.

The output was not static UI screens, but a structured issuance framework that engineering could extend safely.

Governance Layer

To sustain scalability, we implemented a contribution model for new product lines, flow mapping documentation, component usage guidelines, and version control for issuance logic.

In urgent "force-mode" regulatory updates, engineering teams could reuse validated modules, follow defined decision rules, and assemble compliant flows without full UX redesign. UX involvement evolved from execution-heavy to governance-focused.

Launched Features

Advanced Search Redesign

Progressive disclosure search — simple by default, expandable advanced filters aligned with agent mental models. Saved searches for power users. Result preview before submission. Filter adoption more than doubled, average time to locate a record reduced by an estimated 20%.

Data Table System

Redesigned high-density tables: priority columns based on usage analytics, expandable rows for secondary data, consolidated action column, bulk operations, standardized Empty/Loading/Error states. Incorrect record selection reduced by an estimated 25% based on support ticket analysis. Component reused across 4 additional modules.

Navigation Architecture

Card sorting with 8 agents revealed task-based mental models vs. product-based structure. Reorganized navigation by workflow stage, reduced menu depth, defined scalable hierarchy. Adopted as the platform-wide navigation standard, noticeably reducing cross-section navigation.

Organizational Impact

Beyond metrics, the structural impact included reduced UX bottleneck in repetitive flow creation, increased engineering autonomy within defined guardrails, and a shift in perception of Design — from UI production to product infrastructure.

AI-Augmented Document Generation

One of the most time-consuming agent tasks was preparing policy documents — endorsements, renewals, supplementary letters. Agents manually assembled documents by copying templates, adjusting fields, and cross-referencing policy data. The process was error-prone, slow, and inconsistent across branches.

The Problem

  • Wrong template selected for the policy type
  • Outdated clauses copied from old documents
  • Manual data entry errors (policy numbers, dates, amounts)
  • Inconsistent formatting across branches
  • No version control — agents couldn't tell which template was current

Solution

  • Context-aware generation: System auto-selects correct template based on policy type, coverage, and endorsement history
  • Pre-filled data: Policy details pulled automatically from active record — zero manual entry
  • Clause suggestion: AI suggests relevant clauses based on policy type and risk profile — agent reviews and modifies before finalizing
  • One-click generation: Structured preview → confirm → generate final document

Design Rationale

Trust through transparency: Every auto-filled field highlighted. Every suggested clause reviewable, editable, or rejectable. AI assists — never decides unilaterally.

Progressive complexity: Simple documents generated with minimal interaction. Complex documents presented a review step for customization.

Error prevention over correction: Pre-filling from source data and validating against policy rules prevented errors at input — not after generation.

Case Study Overview — Problem, Strategy, Architecture & Outcomes

Platform Overview — Features, Screens & Key Outcomes

"This is not interface optimization. It is product infrastructure design."

The Hook: Users had no mental model for credit inside a telecom app. The challenge wasn't designing a form — it was building financial trust from nothing, across 3 channels, with zero compliance incidents. As the sole designer, I shipped a product that became a new revenue stream.

Context

Hamrah Aval (Iran's largest mobile operator, 50M+ subscribers) wanted to launch a micro-credit service inside its super-app. A true 0→1 initiative — no existing mental model, no precedent within the platform.

The service had three distinct channels, each with different user types, flows, and business rules:

  • B2C: End-user credit request — individuals applying for micro-credit through the app
  • B2B: Business/agent-facing credit operations — different eligibility rules and approval flows
  • CIP: Corporate/institutional credit — higher amounts, stricter compliance, multi-step approval

I was the sole designer responsible for all three channels — from research through shipped product.

The Challenge

This was not a redesign. There was nothing before this. The challenge was creating a financial experience from zero inside an app users associated with phone bills — not banking.

  • Users had no mental model for credit inside a telecom app
  • Strict regulatory requirements — every screen needed compliance approval
  • Risk team imposed rigid eligibility and scoring rules
  • Three channels meant three different user contexts with shared infrastructure
  • Failure would damage trust in the entire super-app ecosystem

Problem Framing

Instead of asking "How do we design a credit request form?", I reframed:

"How might we help users commit to a financial obligation with confidence — inside an app they associate with phone bills, not banking — across three fundamentally different service channels?"

Early usability tests with 8 target users revealed:

  • Users abandoned at terms review — dense legal text prevented comprehension
  • Repayment structure was misunderstood — "total repayment" confused with "monthly installment"
  • Requesting credit ≠ receiving it — lack of state visibility created anxiety

The core barrier was not usability — it was trust and financial clarity.

Design Strategy

  • Transparency First: Show the full obligation before asking for commitment
  • Reduce Decision Anxiety: Visual clarity instead of legal complexity
  • Progressive Commitment: Explore before deciding
  • State Visibility: Every lifecycle stage explicit and trackable
  • Channel Coherence: Shared design language across B2B, B2C, CIP — adapted per context

Key Design Decisions

Early Credit Visibility

Users see approved amount, terms, and total cost before any commitment. No surprises — this single decision addressed the primary trust barrier.

Visual Repayment Breakdown

Replaced wall-of-text terms with structured visual information: installment amount, count, total repayment, and late penalty — each appearing contextually when needed.

Simplified Decision Flow

"Confirm and Receive Credit" — not "Submit." Financial jargon replaced with plain-language equivalents negotiated with Legal. Explicit final confirmation with visual summary.

Decision-State Architecture

Mapped every possible state with Risk: Eligible → Applied → Under Review → Approved → Disbursed → Repaying → Completed + error states (Rejected, Overdue, Suspended). Users always knew where they stood.

Multi-Channel Design System

Designed a shared component architecture that worked across all three channels. B2C used simplified flows, B2B added agent controls, CIP added multi-step approval — all built from the same design foundation.

Product Overview — Credit Management Interface

Case Study — Context, Strategy, Decisions & Impact

"In 0→1, you don't have metrics to improve. You have trust to build from nothing."

The Hook: 8 product modules, each designed by different teams. 14 button styles, 5 color palettes, zero shared components. I showed leadership one Figma frame — the "wall of inconsistency" — and got buy-in the same week. Then built a governed design system that's still alive after I left.

Context

Ewano was the digital services super-app of Hamrah Aval (Iran's largest mobile operator, 50M+ subscribers). The platform included 8 product modules: payments, credit, top-up, bills, marketplace, loyalty, profile management, and support.

Each module had been designed independently by different teams at different times. The result: a fragmented user experience that looked like 8 different products sharing the same app icon.

The Problem

When I joined, a quick visual audit revealed the scale of inconsistency:

9

0

The consequences were real:

  • User trust erosion: Inconsistent patterns made the app feel unreliable — "Is this the same app?"
  • Slow feature delivery: Every new feature required designing components from scratch
  • Developer frustration: Frontend teams implemented the same patterns differently every time
  • Onboarding overhead: New designers and developers had no reference system
  • Design debt accumulating faster than features were shipping

Strategic Framing

The initial request was tactical: "Can you clean up the buttons and make things look consistent?"

I reframed it:

"The problem is not visual inconsistency. The problem is that we have no shared design infrastructure. Without it, every fix is temporary and every new feature adds more debt."

This shifted the initiative from a cosmetic cleanup to an infrastructure project — with buy-in from PM and engineering leadership.

Approach — 4 Phases

Phase 1: Audit & Buy-in

Screenshotted every screen across all 8 modules. Created a "wall of inconsistency" — a single Figma frame showing every button variant, every color, every input style side by side. Presented this to product and engineering leadership.

The visual impact was immediate. No slide deck needed — the wall spoke for itself. Buy-in was granted the same week.

Phase 2: Define — Tokens & Foundations

Established the design foundations before touching any component:

  • Color system: Consolidated 5 blues into 1 primary palette with semantic tokens (primary, success, warning, error, neutral)
  • Typography scale: Defined type ramp with clear hierarchy — heading, subheading, body, caption, overline
  • Spacing system: 4px base grid with defined spacing tokens (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48)
  • Elevation: 4-level shadow system
  • Border radius: Standardized radius tokens

Every token was named semantically — color-primary, not blue-500. This made the system themeable and future-proof.

Phase 3: Build — Atomic Design

Built the component library following atomic design methodology:

  • Atoms: Buttons, inputs, badges, icons, dividers
  • Molecules: Form fields (label + input + validation), list items, card headers
  • Organisms: Forms, data tables, navigation bars, modal sheets, bottom sheets

Every component built with Auto-Layout and Variants in Figma — responsive, state-aware, and production-ready.

Key principle: Every component had to work in RTL — this was not an afterthought but a core requirement from day one.

Phase 4: Govern — Making It Survive

A design system without governance dies when its creator leaves. I built:

  • Usage guidelines: When to use each component, with do/don't examples
  • Contribution process: How to propose new components or modifications
  • Versioning: Semantic versioning for the Figma library
  • Changelog: Documented every update with rationale
  • Review process: New components required design review before library merge

The "Wall of Inconsistency" Technique

This became my most effective communication tool. Instead of explaining the problem in words or slides, I showed it: every variant of every component from every module — side by side on one screen.

When leadership saw 14 different buttons, 5 different blues, and 9 different input fields — all supposedly part of the same product — the conversation shifted from "Is this worth doing?" to "When can you start?"

Challenges & Trade-offs

Migration Strategy

We couldn't stop development to rebuild everything. I designed a progressive migration approach: new features used the system from day one, existing features migrated module by module during maintenance sprints.

RTL Complexity

Every component had to work flawlessly in RTL Persian. Some patterns (progress indicators, carousels, breadcrumbs) required rethinking — not just mirroring.

Developer Adoption

The system was only valuable if frontend actually used it. I paired with developers during initial implementation, created a component mapping document showing Figma component → code component, and held weekly sync sessions during the first two months.

Scope Pressure

Pressure to ship features competed with system work. I protected system time by showing that each system component accelerated future feature work — making the ROI visible sprint by sprint.

Value Created — Before vs. After

The design system didn't just improve consistency — it created new organizational capabilities that didn't exist before:

Before the Design System

  • Every new feature required designing components from scratch
  • Designers spent significant time on repetitive UI decisions instead of product thinking
  • Developers implemented the same patterns differently across modules
  • New team members had no reference — onboarding was slow and inconsistent
  • Every shipped feature added more design debt
  • There was no shared language between design and engineering

After the Design System

  • New features assembled from existing components — design time reduced noticeably
  • Designers focused on product logic and user flows, not button styles
  • Developers had a single source of truth — Figma component → code component mapping
  • New team members onboarded faster with clear reference documentation
  • Every new feature added consistency instead of fragmentation
  • Design and engineering spoke the same component vocabulary

The Real Value: What the System Enabled

The design system's deepest value wasn't efficiency — it was what it unlocked:

  • The Digital Credit Product (0→1) was designed and shipped faster because credit-specific components were built on top of system foundations — not from scratch
  • The B2B, B2C, and CIP channels of the credit product shared components from the system — enabling multi-channel design without multiplying effort
  • When new modules were requested by business, engineering could prototype using existing components before design was even involved
  • The system became the platform's design infrastructure — not a style guide that sits in a folder

Organizational Impact

  • Shared language: Designers and developers started speaking the same component vocabulary
  • Reduced design debt: New features added consistency instead of fragmentation
  • Engineering efficiency: Frontend teams reused coded components instead of re-implementing
  • Design role elevated: Design shifted from "make it look good" to "build product infrastructure"
  • System outlived my involvement: When I transitioned to the Dotin/Pasargad project, the system continued to be used and maintained — the governance model worked

Reflection & Open Questions

This project taught me that a design system is governance architecture, not a component library. The components are the easy part. The hard part is building the processes, documentation, and cultural habits that keep the system alive when you're not in the room.

It also taught me that the value of a design system is not measured in components shipped — it's measured in what it enables others to build without you. When the credit product team could assemble a new channel flow in days instead of weeks because the foundations existed — that's when I knew the system worked.

What I'd challenge: I optimized for adoption speed over documentation depth. The system launched with good governance processes, but the component documentation was functional rather than comprehensive. Teams could use components correctly, but understanding the "why" behind decisions required tribal knowledge. A more thorough documentation-first approach might have slowed initial adoption but created better long-term understanding.

What remains uncertain: The system survived one team transition — but I can't be certain it will survive a second without someone actively championing it. Governance processes are only as strong as the culture that values them. If a new design lead prioritizes speed over system compliance, the governance could erode gradually.
